Riemann: a proof announced with Claude, unverified

Announced, not accepted · 2026-11-20

On 20 November 2026 mathematician Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) posted a 4-page note saying he believes he found, working with Claude, a proof of the Riemann hypothesis and its generalised version, with Lean files. According to AFP and OfficeChai, nobody has verified this: no outside expert has confirmed it, there is no paper, and it is unclear whether the Lean statement faithfully encodes the Riemann hypothesis or whether the files build. We could not read the note itself. The Clay Institute has said nothing, and its rules require peer-reviewed publication followed by two years of scrutiny. Treat it as a claim, not a result.

Riemann: the “quasi-Riemann hypothesis” (OpenAI)

Announced, not accepted · 2026-10-07

In early October 2026 OpenAI released 722 manuscripts from an unreleased internal model, including “The Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis”, about a zero-free region for L-functions. According to Quanta, one version puts the zeros between 1/8 and 7/8 (199 pages, Lean-verified) and another between 1/12 and 11/12 (49 pages, human-edited, not yet Lean-verified); one outlet notes a mismatch between these figures that we could not resolve. Experts largely think the result is correct, but Peter Sarnak will not call it a theorem until humans check the details, and Will Sawin and Thomas Bloom doubt it reaches the 1/2 line. This is not the Riemann hypothesis.

Navier–Stokes: a singularity announced by OpenAI

Announced, not accepted · 2026-09-08

On 8 September 2026 OpenAI announced that a group of AI agents, running on a model that is not public, had found a singularity in the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations. According to Quanta Magazine, the result has been checked in the Lean proof language; humans still have to check that the Lean statement matches what was meant to be proved. This is not an accepted result yet: the Clay Institute still lists the problem as “Active” and has awarded nothing (consulted November 2026), priority is disputed, and we found no peer review. Earlier, in September 2025, a DeepMind-led team used neural networks to find unstable singularities in other equations (porous media, Euler with a boundary), not in Navier–Stokes.

Riemann: more zeros on the critical line (Anthropic)

Progress with AI help · 2026-08-10

On 10 August 2026 Anthropic announced that an unreleased research model had raised the proven lower bound for the proportion of zeta zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. This is a partial result: Anthropic explicitly says it did not prove the Riemann hypothesis and does not expect the technique to resolve it. Anthropic mathematicians checked it, a Lean formalisation is public, and two outside experts (Conrey and Goldston) looked at the paper at short notice. We found no completed peer review.

Erdős problems: what is falling, and the pitfalls

Progress with AI help · 2026-08-03

According to Quanta, amateurs and labs are using models (GPT-5.2, Gemini, an internal OpenAI model) on the Erdős problems. Bloom's database counts, per the article, 565 solved and 652 open problems. Problem 728 was solved in early 2026 with GPT-5.2 Pro and checked with the Aristotle tool, with no prior work found. But problem 333, announced solved at Christmas 2025, had been solved by Erdős himself in 1977, and the announcement was withdrawn. Bloom also says he receives 100- to 200-page texts that “no human has read”. A DeepMind paper quoted by Quanta claims its best agent resolved 9 of 353 open problems (we have not read that paper).

  • Why the Legendary Erdős Problems Are Falling to AI
    Konstantin Kakaes, Quanta Magazine, 2026

Erdős unit distance conjecture: a counterexample announced

Announced, not accepted · 2026-05-20

On 20 May 2026 OpenAI announced that an internal, non-public model had found a counterexample to Erdős's 1946 unit distance conjecture. According to Quanta, the result was not definitive: mathematicians substantially improved it within weeks, and a paper by nine mathematicians comments on the proof's correctness. Quanta quotes Timothy Gowers: had a human submitted it to the Annals of Mathematics, he would have recommended acceptance without hesitation. We found no formal verification; we only read Quanta's account.

Erdős problems: a sweep by Gemini

Progress with AI help · 2026-01-29

Google DeepMind researchers had Gemini evaluate 700 conjectures marked “open” in Thomas Bloom's database of Erdős problems, then had human experts examine the results. Of 13 problems marked open, 5 received seemingly novel autonomous solutions and 8 turned out to be already solved in the literature. The authors warn about the AI's “subconscious plagiarism” and note that these problems were open “through obscurity rather than difficulty”.

  • Semi-Autonomous Mathematics Discovery with Gemini: A Case Study on the Erdős Problems
    Feng, ..., Luong (arXiv preprint), 2026

AlphaEvolve: records in sphere packing and matrix multiplication

Progress with AI help · 2025-05-14

Google DeepMind says AlphaEvolve, a Gemini-powered coding agent, found an algorithm multiplying two 4×4 complex matrices with 48 scalar multiplications (better than Strassen, 1969, in that setting) and a configuration of 593 spheres setting a new lower bound for the kissing number in 11 dimensions. On more than 50 open problems it reportedly matched the state of the art about 75% of the time and improved it in 20%. These are improved bounds, not answers to the questions themselves. Source: the lab's own announcement (not peer-reviewed here; we did not check the associated paper).

  • AlphaEvolve: a Gemini-powered coding agent for designing advanced algorithms
    AlphaEvolve team, Google DeepMind, 2025

AlphaFold: predicting protein shapes

Related work · 2024-10-09

AlphaFold predicts a protein's 3D structure from its sequence. According to DeepMind's page, the CASP14 organisers recognised AlphaFold2 in 2020 as a solution to the “protein folding problem”, and Demis Hassabis and John Jumper were co-awarded the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (with David Baker). Predicting the final shape does not explain how the protein finds it, so the question on the map stays open. Sources: the lab's page only (the Nobel Prize page could not be read).
