What happens to information that falls into a black hole?

Open question

Black holes emit radiation which, according to theory, seems to erase information. This contradicts quantum mechanics.

What we know

  • According to general relativity, a black hole is a region from which nothing comes back, not even light.
  • In 1974–1975 Stephen Hawking showed that, taking quantum physics into account, a black hole emits faint radiation and loses mass, to the point of possibly disappearing.
  • This radiation, as Hawking computed it, is thermal: it does not seem to carry anything of what fell into the black hole.

What we don't know

  • Whether the information about what fell into a black hole is lost for ever, or comes back out in some way. Quantum mechanics says it cannot be destroyed.
  • Calculations since 2019 obtain radiation compatible with the conservation of information. They rely on assumptions and do not settle the whole question.

Why it's hard

  • There is no quantum theory of gravity: the problem arises exactly where relativity and quantum mechanics collide.
  • The radiation of an astrophysical black hole is far too faint to be observed: no experiment can settle it today.

Why it matters

It is a test bench for any theory that would unify gravity and quantum mechanics.

Where to start

  • General relativity, quantum field theory, then quantum gravity.
  • A first step: understand why destroying information would contradict quantum mechanics.

Going further

The black hole information paradox is an unsolved problem in physics and a paradox that appears when the predictions of quantum mechanics and general relativity are combined. The theory of general relativity predicts the existence of black holes that are regions of spacetime from which nothing—not even light—can escape. In the 1970s, Stephen Hawking applied the semiclassical approach of quantum field theory in curved spacetime to such systems and found that an isolated black hole would emit a form of radiation.
