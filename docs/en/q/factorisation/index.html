Can a large number be factored quickly on an ordinary computer?

Open question

No fast algorithm is known, and much of today's encryption relies on this difficulty. A quantum computer could do it quickly (Shor's algorithm).

What we know

  • Every integer greater than 1 can be written uniquely as a product of primes. For small numbers, finding these factors is easy.
  • For very large numbers (hundreds of digits), the best known algorithms on an ordinary computer take a time that explodes with the number of digits.
  • Widely used encryption systems, such as RSA, rely on this difficulty.
  • In 1994 Peter Shor showed that a large enough quantum computer would factor quickly.

What we don't know

  • Whether a fast algorithm exists for an ordinary computer. Nobody knows one, and nobody has shown that none exists.
  • Whether factoring is as hard as the hardest problems in the class NP. It is thought not, without proof.

Why it's hard

  • Proving that no algorithm, even an unknown one, succeeds: we know how to do this for almost no problem.
  • This problem has a special structure that probably makes it easier than NP-complete problems, without us knowing it to be easy.

Why it matters

The security of much of today's encryption. This motivates work on cryptography able to resist quantum computers.

Where to start

  • Complexity theory and number theory.
  • A first step: factor 91, then 8,633 by hand, and see how the effort grows.

Going further

In mathematics, integer factorization is the decomposition of a positive integer into a product of integers. Every positive integer greater than 1 is either the product of two or more integer factors greater than 1, in which case it is a composite number, or it is not, in which case it is a prime number. For example, 15 is a composite number because 15 = 3 · 5, but 7 is a prime number because it cannot be decomposed in this way.
