How does a protein find its shape?

Open question

We can now predict the shape of many proteins from their sequence. How they take it, or how disordered proteins behave, is less well understood.

Draft written with an AI (Claude). The references exist, checked in OpenAlex; the text has not yet been reviewed by a specialist. Who writes what

What we know

  • A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a precise shape. This shape decides its function.
  • Since Anfinsen's experiments in the 1960s and 1970s, we know that the amino acid sequence generally suffices to determine the shape.
  • In 2020–2021 the AlphaFold program, based on machine learning, predicted with high accuracy the shape of many proteins from their sequence alone.

What we don't know

  • How the chain finds its shape in a fraction of a second among an astronomical number of possibilities (“Levinthal's paradox”).
  • How proteins with no fixed shape behave, which nonetheless have a function.
  • How a protein moves, and what each mutation changes.

Why it's hard

  • Predicting the final shape does not say by which path the protein gets there: this path is very fast and hard to observe.
  • A machine-learning program gives a result, not a physical explanation.

Why it matters

Several diseases are associated with misfolded proteins, and designing drugs or new enzymes requires understanding shape and movement.

Where to start

  • Biophysics and structural biology.
  • A first step: understand why a chain of 100 amino acids cannot “try” all possible shapes.
  • The first article below reviews the problem; the second presents AlphaFold.

Going further

Protein folding is the physical process by which a protein, after synthesis by a ribosome as a linear chain of amino acids, changes from an unstable random coil into a more ordered three-dimensional structure. This structure permits the protein to become biologically functional or active.
