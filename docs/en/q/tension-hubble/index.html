Why do two measurements of the universe's expansion disagree?

Open question

The expansion rate measured in the nearby universe differs from the one inferred from the early universe. Measurement error or new physics?

What we know

  • The universe is expanding: the farther a galaxy, the faster it recedes. The ratio between the two is called the Hubble constant.
  • It can be measured in two ways: with stars and exploding stars in the nearby universe, or from the cosmic microwave background, the light emitted about 380,000 years after the start of the universe, relying on a model of the universe.
  • The two methods give different values, about 73 and 67 kilometres per second per megaparsec.

What we don't know

  • Whether the gap comes from unnoticed measurement errors or from still unknown physics.
  • Which of the two values is right.

Why it's hard

  • Both series of measurements have become very precise: the gap is larger than the stated uncertainties.
  • The comparison assumes the standard cosmological model describes the universe well between the microwave background and today. If it is incomplete, the inference is skewed.

Why it matters

If the gap is real, the current cosmological model is incomplete.
