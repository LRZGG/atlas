Where and how did life arise on Earth?

Open question

Almost no trace remains of that era, and nobody has recreated in the lab a system that reproduces itself from simple chemistry.

What we know

  • Life has existed on Earth for a very long time: the oldest fossil micro-organisms observed are dated to at least 3.5 billion years.
  • The building blocks of life (amino acids, components of RNA, lipids) can form under conditions judged plausible for the early Earth. Experiments have shown this, from Miller's in 1953 to the synthesis of RNA components in 2009 (last article below).
  • Several scenarios are studied: an “RNA world”, where RNA stores information and catalyses reactions, hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor, warm surface pools. None is demonstrated.

What we don't know

  • The full path, from lifeless chemistry to a cell able to reproduce and evolve: we know some steps, not how they connect.
  • Where and when it happened, and whether it is a rare event or a probable one.

Why it's hard

  • Almost no rock from that era remains: the oldest geological records are rare and heavily altered.
  • We cannot repeat the experiment over millions of years or in a whole ocean: labs reproduce only isolated steps.
  • These steps often require chemical conditions that are incompatible with each other, which forces us to imagine environments where they follow one another.

Why it matters

Knowing whether life is a rare accident or a probable result of chemistry, which changes how we look for life elsewhere.

Going further

Abiogenesis or the origin of life is the natural process by which life arises from non-living matter, such as simple organic compounds. The prevailing scientific hypothesis is that the transition from non-living to living entities on Earth was not a single event, but a process of increasing complexity involving the formation of a habitable planet, the prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules, molecular self-replication, self-assembly, autocatalysis, and the emergence of cell membranes.
