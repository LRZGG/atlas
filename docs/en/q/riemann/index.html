Is the Riemann hypothesis true?

Open question

Formulated in 1859, it concerns the distribution of prime numbers.

Draft written with an AI (Claude). The references exist, checked in OpenAlex; the text has not yet been reviewed by a specialist. Who writes what

What we know

  • Prime numbers follow a known average law (the prime number theorem, proved in 1896). Deviations from this law are read in the zeros of a function, the Riemann zeta function.
  • In 1859 Riemann asserted that all the “non-trivial” zeros of this function have real part equal to 1/2. This is the Riemann hypothesis.
  • Computers have checked it for all zeros up to a height of about 3,000 billion, with no counterexample.

What we don't know

  • Whether it is true for all zeros, of which there are infinitely many.
  • The exact deviations of the distribution of primes from the average law.

Why it's hard

  • No computer check can cover infinitely many zeros: a proof is needed.
  • For neighbouring objects, the analogue of the hypothesis has been proved (by Weil, then Deligne), but the method has not so far been transposed to the integers.

Why it matters

Many results on primes are proved “if the Riemann hypothesis is true”. It is one of the Millennium Prize problems: a million dollars to whoever solves it.

Going further

In mathematics, the Riemann hypothesis is the conjecture that the Riemann zeta function has its zeros only at the negative even integers and at complex numbers with real part. Many consider it to be the most important unsolved problem in pure mathematics. It is of great interest in number theory because it implies results about the distribution of prime numbers. It was proposed by Bernhard Riemann, after whom it is named.
