Riemann: more zeros on the critical line (Anthropic)

Progress with AI help · 2026-08-10

On 10 August 2026 Anthropic announced that an unreleased research model had raised the proven lower bound for the proportion of zeta zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. This is a partial result: Anthropic explicitly says it did not prove the Riemann hypothesis and does not expect the technique to resolve it. Anthropic mathematicians checked it, a Lean formalisation is public, and two outside experts (Conrey and Goldston) looked at the paper at short notice. We found no completed peer review.

Riemann: the “quasi-Riemann hypothesis” (OpenAI)

Announced, not accepted · 2026-10-07

In early October 2026 OpenAI released 722 manuscripts from an unreleased internal model, including “The Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis”, about a zero-free region for L-functions. According to Quanta, one version puts the zeros between 1/8 and 7/8 (199 pages, Lean-verified) and another between 1/12 and 11/12 (49 pages, human-edited, not yet Lean-verified); one outlet notes a mismatch between these figures that we could not resolve. Experts largely think the result is correct, but Peter Sarnak will not call it a theorem until humans check the details, and Will Sawin and Thomas Bloom doubt it reaches the 1/2 line. This is not the Riemann hypothesis.

Riemann: a proof announced with Claude, unverified

Announced, not accepted · 2026-11-20

On 20 November 2026 mathematician Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) posted a 4-page note saying he believes he found, working with Claude, a proof of the Riemann hypothesis and its generalised version, with Lean files. According to AFP and OfficeChai, nobody has verified this: no outside expert has confirmed it, there is no paper, and it is unclear whether the Lean statement faithfully encodes the Riemann hypothesis or whether the files build. We could not read the note itself. The Clay Institute has said nothing, and its rules require peer-reviewed publication followed by two years of scrutiny. Treat it as a claim, not a result.

What we know

  • Prime numbers follow a known average law (the prime number theorem, proved in 1896). Deviations from this law are read in the zeros of a function, the Riemann zeta function.
  • In 1859 Riemann asserted that all the “non-trivial” zeros of this function have real part equal to 1/2. This is the Riemann hypothesis.
  • Computers have checked it for all zeros up to a height of about 3,000 billion, with no counterexample.

What we don't know

  • Whether it is true for all zeros, of which there are infinitely many.
  • The exact deviations of the distribution of primes from the average law.

Why it's hard

  • No computer check can cover infinitely many zeros: a proof is needed.
  • For neighbouring objects, the analogue of the hypothesis has been proved (by Weil, then Deligne), but the method has not so far been transposed to the integers.

Why it matters

Many results on primes are proved “if the Riemann hypothesis is true”. It is one of the Millennium Prize problems: a million dollars to whoever solves it.

Going further

In mathematics, the Riemann hypothesis is the conjecture that the Riemann zeta function has its zeros only at the negative even integers and at complex numbers with real part. Many consider it to be the most important unsolved problem in pure mathematics. It is of great interest in number theory because it implies results about the distribution of prime numbers. It was proposed by Bernhard Riemann, after whom it is named.
