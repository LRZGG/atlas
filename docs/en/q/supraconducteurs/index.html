Why do some materials conduct without resistance at relatively high temperatures?

Open question

We cannot explain the so-called “high-temperature” superconductors, discovered in 1986.

What we know

  • A superconductor lets electric current through with no resistance at all. Discovered in 1911, it first had to be cooled close to absolute zero.
  • In 1957 BCS theory explained classical superconductors: electrons pair up thanks to the vibrations of the crystal.
  • In 1986 Bednorz and Müller discovered copper oxides that are superconducting at much higher temperatures, which earned them the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1987.

What we don't know

  • Why these materials are superconducting: BCS theory does not apply to them and no explanation is unanimously accepted.
  • Whether a room-temperature, ordinary-pressure superconductor exists. None has been confirmed.

Why it's hard

  • Electrons interact very strongly with one another, which makes exact calculations impossible, even with the biggest computers.
  • These are complex crystals, hard to make cleanly and to describe with a simple model.

Why it matters

Carrying electricity without losses, making powerful magnets that are cheaper to cool, and better understanding matter when electrons interact strongly.

Going further

High-temperature superconductivity is superconductivity in materials with a critical temperature above 77 K, the boiling point of liquid nitrogen. They are "high-temperature" only relative to previously known superconductors, which function only closer to absolute zero. The first high-temperature superconductor was discovered in 1986 by IBM researchers Georg Bednorz and K. Alex Müller.
