What is the biological basis of consciousness?

Open question

What happens in the mind comes from processes in the brain, but we are only beginning to disentangle them.

What we know

  • The brain and consciousness are closely linked: a lesion, anaesthesia or deep sleep changes or removes lived experience.
  • We can identify “neural correlates”: brain activity that accompanies a conscious experience. For example, what differs in the brain between an image we see and the same image we do not notice.
  • Several scientific theories compete to explain it, and researchers are trying to test them against each other.

What we don't know

  • Why neural activity comes with lived experience: the taste of a fruit, the redness of a colour, a pain. The philosopher David Chalmers called this the “hard problem” of consciousness.
  • Which beings have it: some animals? Could a machine? There is no test accepted by everyone.

Why it's hard

  • Lived experience is private: nobody can measure it from outside. We depend on what the person reports, and a baby, an animal or an unresponsive patient cannot report anything.
  • There is no definition of consciousness that everyone agrees on, and competing theories are hard to tell apart by experiment.

Why it matters

Knowing whether an unresponsive patient feels anything, what we owe to animals, and what machines that claim to feel are worth.

Where to start

  • Cognitive neuroscience studies neural correlates; philosophy of mind studies the underlying question. The two meet here.
  • A basic protocol: compare what the brain does for a conscious perception and for one that is not.
  • To set out the problem, Chalmers's text below is the starting point.

Going further

In philosophy of mind, the hard problem of consciousness is to explain how and why organisms have qualia, phenomenal consciousness, or subjective experience. It is contrasted with the "easy problems" of explaining why and how physical systems give rise to the ability to discriminate, to integrate information, and to perform behavioural functions such as watching, listening, speaking, and so forth.
