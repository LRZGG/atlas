How do anaesthetics switch off consciousness?

Open question

They are used every day, but their mechanism of action is not fully understood.

What we know

  • General anaesthetics cause a reversible loss of consciousness. They have been used in surgery since the mid-19th century.
  • They act on several neuronal proteins, notably GABA receptors, and on neural networks involved in sleep and wakefulness.
  • Very different molecules, gaseous or injected, produce similar effects.

What we don't know

  • How these molecular effects lead to loss of consciousness.
  • Why chemically very different substances have similar effects.

Why it's hard

  • Consciousness itself is not explained: so we cannot say exactly what is being switched off.
  • An anaesthetic acts at several levels at once, from molecules to neural networks, which makes its effects hard to isolate.

Why it matters

Anaesthetising more safely, detecting consciousness in a patient who cannot respond, and gaining clues about what consciousness is.

Where to start

  • Cellular neuroscience and the neuroscience of consciousness.
  • A first step: understand how anaesthesia differs from sleep.
  • The two articles below review what is known.

Going further

General anaesthesia (UK) or general anesthesia (US) is drug-induced loss of consciousness that renders a subject unarousable even by painful stimuli. It is achieved through medications, which can be injected or inhaled, often with an analgesic and neuromuscular blocking agent.
