Are there infinitely many twin primes?

Open question

Twin primes differ by 2, like 11 and 13. In 2013 it was proved that there are infinitely many pairs of primes separated by a bounded gap, without being able to bring that gap down to 2.

What we know

  • A prime number has only two divisors: 1 and itself. Two primes are twins when they differ by 2: 3 and 5, 11 and 13, 17 and 19.
  • Primes become rarer as we go on, but twin pairs keep turning up: the largest known have hundreds of thousands of digits.
  • In 2013 Yitang Zhang proved that there are infinitely many pairs of primes separated by less than 70 million. This ceiling was then lowered to 246 thanks to the work of James Maynard and the collaborative Polymath project.

What we don't know

  • Whether there are infinitely many twin primes: the guaranteed gap would have to go from 246 to 2, and current methods are not enough.
  • The fine distribution of primes: we know the average law (the prime number theorem), not the local detail.

Why it's hard

  • Primes are defined by multiplication, while the twin question concerns a gap of addition (2). The two operations combine badly.
  • Sieve methods, which eliminate non-primes, hit a parity obstacle: they distinguish poorly a number with an even number of prime factors from one with an odd number.

Why it matters

Understanding how primes are distributed locally, and not just on average.

Where to start

  • Analytic number theory, and in particular sieve methods.
  • A first step: find twin primes by hand up to 100, then ask what would stop them from ever ending.
  • The two articles below are those of Zhang and Maynard.

Going further

A twin prime is a prime number that is either 2 less or 2 more than another prime number—for example, either member of the twin prime pair or (41, 43). In other words, a twin prime is a prime that has a prime gap of two. Sometimes the term twin prime is used for a pair of twin primes; an alternative name for this is prime twin or prime pair.
