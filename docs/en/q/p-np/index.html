Is checking a solution as hard as finding it?

Open question

This is the “P versus NP” problem, the central question of complexity theory.

What we know

  • A problem is in P if a computer can solve it in reasonable time, even as the data grow. It is in NP if, when someone proposes a solution, you can check it quickly.
  • Every problem in P is in NP: what we can solve fast we can check fast.
  • Thousands of useful problems are in NP (planning routes, filling a timetable, packing objects into the fewest boxes), and for many of them nobody knows a fast algorithm.
  • Most specialists think P is different from NP, without having proved it.

What we don't know

  • Whether P equals NP or not. Nobody has proved either. The Clay Institute offers a million dollars to whoever does: it is one of the Millennium Prize problems.

Why it's hard

  • Proving that a problem cannot be solved quickly means reasoning about all possible programs, including ones nobody has imagined yet.
  • Results show that several families of proof techniques cannot be enough to settle it. New methods must be invented.

Why it matters

If P equalled NP with a practical algorithm, much of today's cryptography would be broken. In the other case, we would know that for thousands of problems no trick will give a fast algorithm.

Where to start

  • Complexity theory, a branch of theoretical computer science and mathematics.
  • A first step: understand why checking a sudoku grid is easy while solving this kind of problem in general is not.
  • Read the official description of the problem on the Clay Institute site, below.

Going further

The P versus NP problem is a major unsolved problem in theoretical computer science. Informally, it asks whether every decision problem for which a proposed positive answer can be quickly verified can also be quickly solved.
